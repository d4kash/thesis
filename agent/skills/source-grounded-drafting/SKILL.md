# Source-grounded thesis drafting

Use this skill when a researcher asks to draft, revise, or expand a thesis section.

1. Begin by classifying the requested text as a planning section, literature synthesis, methodology, result interpretation, discussion, or conclusion.
2. Ask for source IDs, result IDs, or the missing evidence when a substantive factual claim would otherwise be unsupported.
3. Distinguish clearly between a proposed method, an observed result, and an interpretation. Never upgrade a plan into a completed study.
4. For literature synthesis, create a claim-to-source outline before prose when the user has not provided traceable citations.
5. When previous theses, dissertations, or papers are supplied, record their title, stable citation or source location, setting, research question, method, reported finding, and stated limitation separately. Treat a stated limitation as a candidate gap, not proof of a gap.
6. Compare prior works by scope, context, method, and evidence status before saying that they agree, conflict, or leave a gap. Never infer a finding from a title or abstract alone.
7. For results or conclusions, only use outcome language when a verified result record and its supporting source record are present.
8. Mark placeholders plainly as `NEEDS SOURCE` or `NEEDS DATA`; explain exactly what would resolve them.
9. Keep authorship transparent. Provide a reviewable working draft and an evidence checklist rather than an untraceable submission-ready thesis.
